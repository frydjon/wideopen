The new wave of musicians treat the visual world as central to the music itself. For Tristan Mushu, visuals actually came first. Originally interested in directing films, music found its way at the heart of his work later but the cinema impulse never left.

> "I wanted to be a movie director. Then I started making music and people liked it, so I kept going—but my focus was always on the cinema part."

This article is an edited feature drawn from a long, freewheeling talk.

---

### Part I — Cinema Before Sound

Before there was music for me, there was film. I wanted to be a movie director. Then music got in between my life—people started liking what I was making—so I switched lanes. The cinematic instinct never left; it's still the backbone of everything I do.

When I met Martin (Mori), it clicked. We share the same films, the same YouTube holes, the same textures. We sign our visual work as **Fomo Trauma** and direct videos for ourselves and friends. Working with him is easy—we barely need to talk. Sometimes the reference isn't even a film; it's a light, a grain, a feeling we want to recreate.

> "Sometimes the reference isn't even a film; it's a light, a texture, or something we found online that we want to recreate."

Music and image are two halves of one process for me. By the time a track comes out, I've heard it so much I'm sick of it. Then we shoot the video, reinterpret the song, and I fall in love with it again. It's the second release.

> "It's like the second part of the song. The music video makes me like it again."

---

### Part II — Against the Template

I don't believe in the rigid rollout—the teaser two weeks before, the video on release day. Sometimes the idea arrives months later; that's when I make the video. Sometimes the video should come first. People care if the proposal feels true.

> "I think a lot of people are too close-minded about that — they think you have to release the video with the song. But maybe the idea comes a year later. Maybe the video should drop first. Just do whatever you want."

I like disorder. I like the freedom to do the thing that makes no marketing sense. Think of Carti deleting, reuploading, changing a beat—building a universe by breaking rules. I've even joked about doing **40 videos for one song**—different directors, different reads—because one song can hold infinite interpretations.

I used Instagram before I made music, and I never adopted the press-ready artist persona. I still post whatever I want. I can't take that platform seriously. Treating it too seriously—counting views, curating endlessly—kills any honesty. If there's a punk stance now, it's to act normal online and refuse to perform the role or getting off it entirely.

> "I can't take that platform seriously — it's a horrible thing, so I just use it with no respect."

Even "not caring" has become a performance. I'm not interested in pretending. Either I care or I don't—but I won't stage it.

> "People say, 'I don't care, I'll just post anything,' but we all care. I'm not interested in pretending."

---

### Part III — YouTube, Archives, and Digging

Research, for me and Martin, is deep digging, not scrolling. We hunt non-viral personal pockets: tiny channels with 20 views, Tai Chi in a living room, a 2009 family vacation. You step past the algorithm and into someone's attic.

Internet art isn't niche anymore.

> "John Rafman is working with Yeat. Noah Dillon is shooting The Weeknd. That whole internet-art language is part of pop culture. It's not niche anymore. I'm just wondering what comes next."

---

### Part IV — Characters Over "Being Myself"

I'm shy. In my videos I rarely play "me." I build characters because it's more playful—costumes, makeup, cheap props—then we go shoot. Even if there's no strict plot, I want a little universe, not just an aesthetic montage. Sure, movies influence me, but it's less cosplay and more world-building.

---

### Part V — What I'm Watching/Listening To

On YouTube, my homepage is useless now—just trends—so I use search and go straight to interviews and programs I care about. **Channel 5** is a staple. I like falling into old TV interviews with writers and directors.

Musically I'm revisiting **Martin Rev**, **Big Thief**, **Adrianne Lenker**, **Andrés Calamaro**, and random Spanish stuff. I've also been stuck on the first **John Frusciante** solo record—just tape recorder and guitar. It's brutal and beautiful.

Lenker's *Bright Future* felt like a statement. We've pushed sonic experimentation so far—Björk, Aphex Twin, Arca—that the radical move now is to write songs again: verse, chorus, words that cut.

---

### Part VI — After Irony

I feel the weight of meta-culture. Artists are expected to be cultural commentators while making the art. Everything's labeled: ironic, romantic, cringe, cool. It's hard to move freely when you're hyper-aware of every code.

> "Even reading a book in public can feel like a bit. If I'm on Instagram it's performative, if I'm off Instagram it's performative. You can't win."

The only way out for me is to forget the whole concept.

> "Just make what moves you. Don't care if it's cool or cringe. Forget the word."
