In late capitalism, the referent has vanished. Marketing no longer refers to real qualities of products, but aestheticized moods.

Scholars generally state that this happened in the 1950s-1970s. TV created new ways of image-based marketing, rather than text-based product descriptions. Three examples:

1. **Marlboro cigarettes (1950s-60s):** The Marlboro Man campaign did not market cigarettes on taste or quality. Instead, it associated Marlboros with ruggedness, masculinity, and freedom.

2. **Coca-Cola (1971):** The I'd Like To Buy The World A Coke campaign contained no information about the actual product. It was about peace and harmony while drinking Coca-Cola.

3. **Nike (late 1980s):** The Just Do It campaign did not discuss shoe quality or features. It purely sold determination and athletic achievement.

This was part of a transition from an industrial to a post-industrial economy in the West, where symbolic and cultural production was becoming more important than material production. Today, the transition is complete. We call it the vibe economy.

This is old news. Jean Baudrillard had already fully articulated this in 1981 in *Simulacra and Simulation*, arguing that society had moved beyond representing reality to a state where representations only refer to *other* representations. He called this *hyperreality*.

The Millennial and post-Millennial generations have grown up entirely under hyperreality. Case in point: I was born in 1996. My discovery of 'skate culture' -- something that originally emerged in Southern California in the 50s -- came solely from *Tony Hawk's Pro Skater*. That was also how I discovered punk rock and, of course, Vans shoes. But skate culture, punk rock, and Vans had already gone through several stages of representation.

Let's look at this through Baudrillard's four stages of simulation.

1. *Sign reflects basic reality* (1950s): Surfers created "sidewalk surfing" to practice when waves were flat. Sidewalk surfing became "skating", something to do in empty pools in drought-stricken California. Skateboarding directly represented lived reality.

2. *Sign distorts reality* (1980s): Skateboarding became commercialized through companies like Powell-Peralta. The rebellious nature was partially preserved, but packaged for wider consumption.

3. *Sign masks the absence of reality* (1990s): With the X-Games and corporate sponsorships, skateboarding became a mainstream spectacle. Professional competitions with standardized formats replaced the organic, location-specific creativity of early skating. The aesthetic remained, but the original context disappeared.

4. *Sign bears no relation to reality whatsoever* (2000s): Tony Hawk's Pro Skater created a completely simulated skateboard experience -- physics defied reality, locations were cartoonish composites, and achievements were measured by points. Players could experience skateboarding without ever touching a real board.

Critically, it is at this stage that *hyperreality* happens --- the game itself became a reference point for real skaters, creating a loop where reality imitates the simulation.

We see this in both punk rock and Vans shoes. Punk was originally a direct reflection of working-class alienation during the economic crises of the 1970s. Vans shoes were originally a functional product to maximize grip on boards. Today, both are decontextualized. Pop-punk is in teen movies and commercials to simulate rebelliousness. Limited releases and collaborations have disconnected Vans' utilitarian origins, making them objects of consumer desire. You can have a white picket fence life and make "punk music". You can have a connection to what skate culture *represents*, without requiring any physical participation.

This is not just about skateboarding. Simulations are incredibly powerful, and they are everywhere today. They extend beyond the co-option of ideas like "ruggedness", "peace", "rebelliousness", or "determination". They affect our most intimate relationships.

Think about how people re-enact proposals from TV shows or design weddings for Instagram. When this person imagines love, they are imagining a scenario: a scene, a tone, or a set of gestures already seen and felt through media. The end result is that one relates to another through *inherited codes of cinematic narratives: meet-cute, tension, climax, resolution*. We have entire generations of people performing love as we've seen it, often in order to *feel that it is real*.

This is not a moral judgment against people, nor simulations. In some cases, people today encounter the cultural elements first through the fourth stage (pure simulation), and work backward to discover the earlier, more authentic stages. Simulations can preserve a cultural form that could have otherwise disappeared or be geographically inaccessible.

But one must also weigh it against a critical perspective. What if you don't put in the time to 'work backwards'?

There are two main concerns.

The first, as Baudrillard noted, is that simulations flatten political and social contexts. A punk song divorced from its protest context is *mere aesthetic*. Intimacy in a vacuum -- "two lovers against the world" -- strips away the importance of community embeddedness, turning romance itself into a *consumable product*.

The second concern is more straightforward. It's also much more worrying.

*Who controls the simulation?*

The traditional answer: media conglomerates.

Large corporations like Disney, Meta, and other media giants have enormous power to shape which cultural elements get simulated, and who can access them. Their decisions determine which aspects of culture get amplified or marginalized.

It's why we have been living in what is called the "culture wars". Content decisions are now flashpoints in cultural debates. And let's be real: even when corporations seem to take a progressive stance, i.e. diverse casting of characters, this is simply a calculated move for maximizing shareholder value, as is their legal obligation.

Rainbow capitalism means corporate adoption of Pride while donating to politicians who support anti-LGBTQ+ legislation.

Naomi Klein's *No Logo*, published in 1999, expanded on this significantly. But today's answer on "who controls the simulation has taken a technological bent", and one that cuts into how we regulate the deepest parts of ourselves.

Today, algorithms control the simulation.

It's common knowledge that algorithmic amplification is a huge determinant in which social movements gain traction.

For example, we know that Facebook and YouTube's algorithms give more weight to "angry" reactions, amplifying divisive content.

Obviously, the algorithms themselves do not 'prefer' anger in any ideological sense.

Instead, it is part of an *affective* feedback loop: divisive content -> engagement -> algorithmic promotion -> further division.

The conclusion is that human emotional volatility is used for monetization. This phenomenon can be described as a structural *exploitation of affect*.

The exploitation of affect means that in the same way as Just Do It campaign wasn't just selling shoes, algorithmic platforms are not just selling content. In fact, they are not selling anything at all.

Instead, they are organizing experiences around affective tags -- categories of mood. Streaming platforms have a seemingly endless number of mood playlists, from the notorious "Lo-Fi Chill" playlist to "Coming of Age". People put them on to feel a "type of vibe".

*Listen to X -> Feel Y.*

It is a system that assumes a linear relationship between sign and experience. But emotional response is never guaranteed: an "upbeat" song might provoke grief when it reminds you of your dead spouse. A "sad" film might induce calm.

It does not matter how gracefully an artist gestures or showcases their media literacy. *Affect is not transmitted*. Nothing can guarantee an emotional result. No medium -- music, film, painting, architecture, literature, or even ritual -- can transmit emotions as a *fixed payload*. To believe one could transmit emotion is to take a pornographic approach to creation -- one's goal would be merely to engineer a physiological response.

I once met someone who was trying to contrive a song to fit exactly onto a "lo-fi coming of age" playlist. This led to the creation of nothing interesting whatsoever. Emotional experiences are not inside objects. Emotional experiences occur in the encounter between subject and object.

But when affect is prepackaged as a payload -- songs designed for a playlist, images to trend on a Pinterest board, we are basically accepting the statement: *"feel this now."*

There is no space for emotional ambiguity, and therefore the emotional texture that comes with a contradictory experience. This poses the question: if every emotional experience can be prefigured by an algorithm, what does this do to people?

## Post Script

Below are three possible views:

**View A (Liberal awareness)** - *Our emotional experiences exist on a spectrum of autonomy. Sometimes they are algorithmically influenced, sometimes spontaneous and unexpected. The key is to develop greater awareness of these influences and deliberately create space for emotional experiences that transcend algorithmic categorization.*

**View B (Extropian)** - *Algorithmic curation of emotion marks humanity's most sophisticated evolution in emotional intelligence. Throughout history, humans have used external tools to shape and enhance emotional experiences—from religious rituals to novels to music. Algorithmic systems are the next logical step in this progression, offering unprecedented personalization and discovery.*

**View C (Anti-extropian)** - *Algorithmic emotion represents nothing less than the final colonization of human interiority by capital. When our emotional experiences are mediated through algorithmic systems, we aren't experiencing emotions on a "spectrum of autonomy" -- we're experiencing emotional alienation masquerading as choice.*

Personally, I believe View A is a non-answer, as it puts the onus entirely on an individual person to be 'aware' of how they are influenced by what they consume, ignoring the fact that people don't live in vacuums.

View B is a dark futurology that assumes a "linear progression" of human 'development'. It completely ignores that algorithmic prescription of affect was specifically designed to increase profits within a capitalist attention economy. It is extremely naive to be complacent about the for-profit corporations that control these systems, especially given how little accountability they have been given.

View C is the closest to capturing this phenomenon and problematizing it. I believe the solution requires recognition that emotions are neither purely personal possessions nor simply imposed structures. They are also emergent properties of complex socio-technical systems. What do we do with that? I don't know.
