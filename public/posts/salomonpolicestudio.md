Salomon operates across multiple sport verticals—from snowboarding to lifestyle—creating a challenge to unify these disparate segments under one visual identity. The rebranding process took nearly two years.

## Part I: Salomon and the Design of a Complex Brand

Police Studio began with typography, a core competency. As one of the first Paris studios to consistently create custom typefaces for clients, they leveraged improved font-creation software to expand this offering beyond luxury brands.

For Salomon, typography became the storytelling vehicle. The designers created a dual type family—serif and sans-serif versions—to express the tension between the brand's fashion-adjacent lifestyle positioning and its sporting heritage. As the founders note, "typography as a voice" allows brands to communicate multiple narratives simultaneously.

The design process employed "system boards" rather than single-direction moodboards. These presentations showed logos, taglines, and typographic elements across multiple narrative directions, enabling rapid decision-making.

DDB Paris served as creative partner, handling marketing strategy while Police Studio applied design execution. This collaborative approach proved pragmatic and fruitful, with the agency granting substantial creative autonomy.

## Part II: Philosophy, Typography, and Studio Origins

Police Studio emerged over a decade ago while founders were still in design school at Olivier de Serres. Recognizing a gap in typography-focused studios, they built their identity around custom font creation. The name "Police" references the French word for typeface.

Early clients came from hospitality—restaurants discovering design value. These modest budgets allowed independence from investors while developing expertise. Even small projects included bespoke typography, building portfolio strength.

Graphic design for restaurants involves practical constraints: daily menu printing by staff, material durability requirements, and limited budgets. These constraints informed the studio's design philosophy.

The founders rejected trend-following, preferring hand-drawn sketches and book-based research over algorithmic inspiration. "Hand-drawing over scrolling" remains their methodology, acknowledging how platforms like Are.na can create homogenized visual loops.

## Part III: HARK, Logo Neutrality, and Creative Legacy

HARK—comprising a vinyl record store, high-end speaker manufacturer, and repair service—presented similar branding challenges to Salomon.

The designers created a timeless identity echoing vintage audio hardware aesthetics. However, they challenged the industry trend toward "logo neutrality"—the belief that minimal, generic branding ensures longevity.

"Find their own voice" becomes the studio's consistent client directive. This need not be loud or complex, but distinctiveness drives meaningful design. The founders critique the interchangeable quality of contemporary tech logos: "You've probably seen those images where all the big tech or startup brands' logos are lined up and they all look the same."

The modernist "less is more" principle has reached its limits. Drawing from scratch, referencing design heritage, and allowing narrative to guide form creates lasting visual identities that evolve without becoming generic.
